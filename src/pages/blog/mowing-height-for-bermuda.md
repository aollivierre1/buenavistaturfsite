Mowing height is the setting people fiddle with most and understand least. Bermuda has a reputation as a grass you can cut short, which is true, and that gets taken to mean shorter is always better, which is not.

## The range Bermuda is happy in

Bermuda is a low-growing, spreading grass, and it tolerates being cut shorter than most lawn grasses without complaint. For a home lawn kept with a standard rotary mower, somewhere in the lower-to-middle part of your mower's range suits it well through the growing season.

Golf courses and sports fields cut Bermuda far shorter than that, which is where the "cut it low" idea comes from. What usually goes unmentioned is everything that goes with it: specialist reel mowers, cutting several times a week, and a feeding and watering programme to match. Cutting a home lawn to those heights with a rotary mower and a weekend schedule does not produce a golf green. It produces a stressed lawn.

The practical answer is to pick a height in Bermuda's comfortable range and then stay there. Consistency does more good than finding a perfect number.

## The one-third rule

This is the rule that matters more than the height itself: never take off more than about a third of the blade in a single cut.

The leaf is where the plant makes its food. Removing most of it at once forces the grass to rebuild from reserves instead of growing, and a lawn that keeps having to do that gets thinner over a season rather than denser. It also opens the canopy enough for sunlight to reach soil, which is an invitation to weeds.

In practice this sets your mowing frequency rather than the other way around. In peak growing season Bermuda can put on enough height in a week that a single cut takes far more than a third. If that is happening, the lawn needs cutting more often — not a lower setting to make up for it.

If the lawn has got away from you and is much too long, bring it down over two or three cuts a few days apart rather than scalping it back in one pass.

## Why lower is not always better

Cutting height and root depth are connected. A taller canopy supports a deeper root system, and a deeper root system is what carries the lawn through heat and dry spells.

Cut very short, Bermuda has less leaf to photosynthesise with, shallower roots, and bare soil exposed to direct sun. It dries faster, it needs watering more often, and it has less in reserve when conditions get hard. That is a fine trade on a sports field with full-time attention. On a home lawn in a North Texas summer it is a bad trade.

Letting the lawn carry a little more height as real heat arrives is one of the simplest things you can do for it. More leaf means more shade over its own soil, and soil that stays cooler and holds moisture longer.

## Sharp blades

A dull blade does not cut grass, it tears it. The difference is easy to see a day or two after mowing: a cleanly cut lawn looks even, while a torn one has a whitish, frayed cast across the tips where every blade is drying out from a ragged wound.

Torn tips also lose more water and give disease an easier entry. Sharpening once or twice through a mowing season is not a detail, it is most of the difference between a lawn that looks cared for and one that does not.

## What to do with clippings

Leave them, as long as you are mowing often enough that they are short.

Short clippings break down quickly and return nitrogen and organic matter to the soil. It is free feeding, and it reduces what the lawn needs from anywhere else. They do not cause thatch — that comes from the stems and runners below, not from leaf clippings.

The exception is when you have let the lawn get long and are taking off a heavy cut. Clumps sitting on top of the canopy block light and hold damp, so those are worth collecting or spreading out.

## In short

Pick a sensible height, keep it there, cut often enough to respect the one-third rule, keep the blade sharp, and let the clippings fall. That is the whole of it, and it will do more for a Bermuda lawn than any amount of adjusting the deck up and down through the season.
