August is when the calls about brown patches start. The lawn was fine, and now there is an area that is not, and the instinct is to do something about it immediately. The trouble is that the four common causes want four different responses, and treating the wrong one usually makes it worse.

So before reaching for anything, work out what you are looking at.

## Drought stress

This is the most common by a wide margin.

It shows up first where you would expect: the sunniest part of the yard, strips next to paving or a driveway that hold heat, high spots, slopes where water runs off before it soaks in, and any stretch an irrigation head does not quite reach. The edges are soft and vague rather than sharp, and the colour fades through a dull grey-green before it browns rather than turning suddenly.

Walk on it. If your footprints stay flattened, it is short of water.

The fix is to water properly — a long, deep soak rather than a light daily run — and to work out why that area dries faster than the rest. Very often it is coverage, and the answer is a sprinkler adjustment rather than anything you put on the grass.

## Dormancy

If the lawn is going off colour broadly and fairly evenly, rather than in distinct patches, it may be going dormant.

Bermuda does this in summer as well as winter. Faced with sustained heat and not enough water, it stops growing, browns off and waits. It is the same protection it uses in January, and an established lawn can hold that way for a good while and green up again when water returns.

The giveaway is uniformity. Dormancy affects whole areas that share the same conditions, not shapes within otherwise healthy grass. Dormant Bermuda also still has firm, pale crowns at the base of the plant — pull a few sprigs and look where they meet the soil.

The response is to decide which lawn you want for the rest of the summer: water it consistently and keep it green, or let it ride dormant and leave it alone. What is hardest on the grass is bouncing between the two.

## Physical damage

Damage has edges. That is the tell.

A rectangle where something sat for a week. A stripe where a mower wheel ran the same line repeatedly. A scraped area where the deck caught a high spot and scalped it. A worn path across the middle of the yard. Spots from a pet. A chemical or fuel spill.

These show a shape that nothing in nature would make, and they appear within healthy grass rather than fading into it. They also usually appeared quickly, where drought browns gradually.

Watering will not fix damage. In the growing season Bermuda will usually spread back in on its own once whatever caused it stops — it is a spreading grass and filling its own gaps is what it does best. What it needs is time and for the cause to be removed.

## Disease

Least common of the four on a home lawn, but worth recognising.

Disease tends to make distinct shapes — roughly circular patches, or irregular areas that spread outward over days — and it often shows at the individual blade rather than just as overall colour. Lesions, banding, or blades dying from the tip down while the base stays green are all signs that this is not simply dryness.

Disease also has a cause worth looking at. Grass that stays wet overnight is the usual culprit, which is why evening watering is the habit most likely to create it. Too much nitrogen pushing soft growth in heat is another.

If it genuinely looks like disease, the first thing to change is the watering time, not the lawn.

## Working through it

Start with the three cheap checks. Is it a shape, or is it a fade? Did it arrive suddenly, or over weeks? Does the area spring back when you walk on it?

A soft-edged, gradual, non-springy patch in the hottest part of the yard is dry. A sharp-edged shape that appeared fast is damage. A broad even fade across whole sections is dormancy. A spreading circle with marks on individual blades is worth a closer look.

Most August brown is the first two, and most August brown fixes itself with better watering and a little patience. The one thing worth avoiding is fertilising a struggling lawn in peak heat to try to green it up. A plant already rationing water cannot support a flush of new growth, and pushing it is how a stressed lawn becomes a damaged one.
