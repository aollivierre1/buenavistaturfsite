Spring watering is forgiving because the weather helps. Summer is not. Once the rain stops and the heat settles in, whatever the lawn gets is whatever you give it, and the habits that were merely inefficient in May start showing up as brown grass in July.

## What the lawn is up against

By midsummer a Bermuda lawn is carrying a full canopy, growing hard, and losing water through every blade all day. Add long hours of direct sun, air that pulls moisture out of everything, and soil that can dry several inches down between waterings, and the demand is nothing like it was two months ago.

The good news is that Bermuda is built for this. It is one of the better warm-season grasses for heat and drought, and an established lawn with a decent root system will take a summer here in its stride. What it will not take is a shallow root system, and that is usually something created earlier in the year by watering too lightly and too often.

## Deep soaks, not daily sprinkles

The principle does not change from spring, it just matters more.

A couple of long, thorough waterings a week will do far more than a short run every morning. The long soak gets moisture down where the roots are and encourages them to stay there. The short daily run wets the top of the soil, most of it leaves again by midday, and the roots never have any reason to go deep.

A simple check: after you water, push a screwdriver or a long probe into the ground. It should slide in easily for several inches. If it stops short, the water did not get far enough down and the session needs to be longer, not more frequent.

Spread the sessions out rather than bunching them. The soil should dry down between waterings — that cycle of wet and dry is what builds a root system rather than a mat.

## Water early

Early morning, before the sun gets up, is the only time worth watering in summer.

Water at midday and a good share of it evaporates before it does any good. Water in the evening and the lawn sits damp through a warm night, which is the condition that turf disease waits for all year.

Early morning gets water into the soil while it is cool, and the blades dry as the day warms. If you have an irrigation controller and change nothing else this summer, move the start time earlier.

## Reading the lawn

The signals are the same as in spring, they just arrive faster.

**Colour first.** Bermuda under stress goes a duller, greyer green before it browns. That is the moment to water, not after it turns.

**Then footprints.** Walk across and look back. If your tracks stay flattened instead of springing up, the lawn is short of water now.

**Then curling.** Blades folding in along their length are conserving what they have. That is a lawn well past asking.

Watch for the parts of the yard that show it first — usually the sunniest stretch, ground near paving that radiates heat, or a slope where water runs off before it soaks in. Those spots tell you what the rest of the lawn is heading for.

## When it goes brown anyway

In a hard summer, a Bermuda lawn that runs short of water will go dormant. It browns, it stops growing, and it waits. This is the same survival mechanism it uses in winter, and an established lawn can sit that way for a surprisingly long stretch and come back when water returns.

That is worth knowing because it changes what a brown patch in August means. It is usually not a dead lawn. It is a lawn protecting itself.

What you should not do is swing between extremes — letting it go dormant, drowning it for a few days, then letting it go again. Repeatedly pulling a lawn in and out of dormancy is harder on it than either staying watered or staying dormant.

## Keep the mowing honest

Watering and mowing work together in summer. A lawn cut a little taller shades its own soil, which keeps the ground cooler and holds moisture longer, so the water you put down lasts.

And do not cut a lawn that is already drought-stressed. It has nothing in reserve to recover with, and mowing is one more demand on a plant that is already rationing.

## The short version

Water deeply, a couple of times a week, early in the morning, and let the lawn dry between. Watch colour and footprints rather than the calendar. Keep the mower a notch higher than you would in spring. Done that way, a Bermuda lawn gets through a North Texas summer without drama.
