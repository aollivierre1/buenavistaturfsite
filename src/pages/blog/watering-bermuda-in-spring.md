Spring is when most people start watering again, and it is also when most people start watering wrong. The lawn is greening up, it looks like it needs help, and the easiest thing to do is turn the system on and leave it running. More water early almost never produces the lawn anybody is hoping for.

## What a waking lawn is doing

Coming out of dormancy, Bermuda is spending its energy on roots and new growth from the crowns. It is not yet carrying the full canopy it will have in July, so it is not yet losing water at anything like the summer rate. A lawn in April and May is simply using less than it looks like it should.

At the same time, spring is usually the wettest stretch of the year here. Between what falls and what is already in the soil, an established Bermuda lawn often needs very little supplemental water at all for the first several weeks of the season.

## Deep and infrequent beats light and daily

This is the single habit worth building, and spring is the time to build it.

Watering a little every day keeps the top inch of soil damp and nothing below it. Roots have no reason to go looking for moisture, so they stay shallow — and a shallow-rooted lawn is the one that collapses first when real heat arrives in July. You are not just wasting water, you are training the lawn to be fragile.

Watering deeply and less often does the opposite. A longer soak pushes moisture further down, and roots follow it. By the time summer arrives, a lawn watered this way has a root system that can reach water a daily-sprinkled lawn cannot.

In practice that means fewer, longer sessions rather than short ones every morning. Let the surface dry between waterings. The grass should be mildly thirsty before you water it again.

## Reading the grass instead of the calendar

A schedule set in April does not know what the weather is doing in May. The lawn does, and it will tell you.

**Colour.** Bermuda under water stress loses its bright green and takes on a duller, slightly blue-grey cast before it browns. That shift is the early signal, and it shows up well before any damage.

**Footprints.** Walk across the lawn and look back. Grass with enough water springs back and your path disappears. Grass that is short of water stays flattened and you can see exactly where you walked. This is the most reliable check there is, and it costs nothing.

**The soil itself.** Push a screwdriver into the ground. If it goes in easily, there is moisture down there. If you have to lean on it, there is not.

Any one of those tells you more than a timer does.

## Timing within the day

Early morning is the right window. Watering before the heat comes up means more of it reaches the roots instead of evaporating, and the blades dry as the day warms.

Evening watering leaves grass wet through the night, which is the condition turf disease likes best. Midday watering mostly feeds the air. If you only change one thing about how you water, make it the hour.

## When rain does the job

The most common spring mistake is running an irrigation system through a wet week because that is what the schedule says. A lawn sitting in saturated soil is not better off than a dry one — roots need air as well as water, and ground that never drains invites problems that are harder to fix than dryness.

If it has rained meaningfully, skip the cycle. Turning a controller off for a week in spring is not neglect.

## The point of getting spring right

Everything a Bermuda lawn does in August depends on the root system it built in April and May. A lawn trained to find its own water goes into summer with depth to draw on. A lawn trained to expect a drink every morning goes into summer with nothing underneath it, and it is the one that browns first and recovers slowest.

Spring is the cheapest time to get this right, because the weather is doing most of the work for you.
