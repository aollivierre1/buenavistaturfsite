Every year around this time the phone starts ringing about brown lawns. The grass looked fine in September, and now most of the yard has gone the colour of straw. It is the most common winter question we get, and the answer is almost always the same: the lawn is dormant, not dead.

## What dormancy actually is

Bermuda is a warm-season grass. It grows when soil is warm and light is long, and it shuts down when neither of those is true. Once soil temperatures drop and the days shorten, the grass stops putting energy into blades it cannot support and pulls back into the crowns and roots below the surface.

The brown you are looking at is the top growth giving up. The living part of the plant is underneath, holding on until conditions come back. This is not the lawn failing. It is the lawn doing exactly what it is built to do, and it is the same reason Bermuda handles a North Texas summer: it knows how to wait.

## How to tell dormant from dead

The useful signal is uniformity.

A dormant lawn browns evenly. The whole area, or whole sections that get similar light and water, go off colour together over a few weeks as the cold settles in. It looks consistent, even if it looks bad.

A dead patch is a patch. It has edges. It sits inside grass that is a different colour, or it turned brown well before everything around it did, back when the rest of the lawn was still growing. Damage from a leak, a spill, something parked on the grass, or a mower set far too low tends to show up this way.

If you want to check, pull a few sprigs from a brown area and look at the base of the plant where it meets the soil. Dormant Bermuda still has firm, pale crowns and roots that hold on when you tug. Dead grass comes away easily and the base is dry and brittle all the way through.

## What to leave alone

Most winter lawn problems are caused by trying to fix dormancy.

**Do not fertilise.** The grass is not growing and cannot use it. What it does feed is the cool-season weeds that are perfectly happy in this weather, and you will see the result in February.

**Do not keep watering on a summer schedule.** A dormant lawn uses very little. Cooler air, shorter days and winter rain usually cover it. Ground that stays wet through a cold stretch does the lawn no favours.

**Do not try to green it up early.** Nothing you put down in December brings Bermuda out of dormancy. Soil temperature and day length decide that, and they are not negotiable.

## What is worth doing

Keep the leaves off it. A dormant lawn is not growing, but the crowns still want light and air, and a thick wet mat of leaves sitting on them all winter is the one genuinely avoidable problem of the season. A thin scatter can be mulched in with the mower. A heavy layer is better cleared.

Go easy on traffic. Dormant grass does not repair itself, so a path worn across the lawn in January will still be there in April waiting for the growing season to fill it in. The same goes for anything parked or stored on the grass over the winter.

Beyond that, there is not much to do, and that is the point. A dormant Bermuda lawn is low maintenance because the plant has already done the work of protecting itself.

## When it comes back

Green-up happens when soil warms consistently, not on the first warm afternoon. It is gradual, and it is patchy at the start — sunnier, better-drained parts of the lawn wake up before shaded or low spots, and for a few weeks the yard looks uneven. That is normal too.

If an area is still brown well after everything around it has greened up and filled in, that is the point at which it is worth a proper look. Until then, the brown is just winter.
