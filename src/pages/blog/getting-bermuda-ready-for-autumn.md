Autumn is the easiest season to get wrong, because nothing is obviously going wrong. The lawn looks fine, the heat has broken, and the temptation is to carry on doing exactly what you have been doing since June. What the grass actually needs is for several of those things to change.

## The lawn is winding down

As nights cool and days shorten, Bermuda starts slowing. Growth eases off, the lawn needs cutting less often, and the plant begins moving its energy from top growth into crowns and roots — building the reserves it will live on through dormancy.

That shift is the whole point of autumn. Everything worth doing now supports it, and most autumn mistakes work against it by pushing the lawn to keep growing when it is trying to store instead.

## Ease off the mowing, but do not stop

You will notice you are cutting less often. Follow the lawn rather than the calendar: keep mowing while it is still growing, just at whatever interval it now needs.

The one-third rule still applies. It is easier to honour in autumn because growth is slower, but a mild spell can still put on more height than you expect, so keep an eye on it rather than assuming the season is over.

Letting the lawn carry a little more height into cool weather is sensible. A slightly taller canopy supports the root system it is busy building and gives the crowns some cover. What you want to avoid is the opposite extreme in either direction — a long matted lawn going into winter, or a lawn scalped bare right before it goes dormant.

## Keep watering while it is still warm

This is the one people drop too early.

Cooler air does not mean the soil is wet. Autumn here can stay warm and dry for weeks, and a lawn still growing and still building roots still needs moisture to do it. Cutting the water off in September because it no longer feels like summer leaves the grass short of what it needs during the exact period it is preparing for winter.

Keep the same approach — deep and infrequent, early in the day — and simply reduce the frequency as demand drops and the rain picks up. Use the same checks you have used all year: colour first, footprints second. Taper it off rather than switching it off.

## Go easy on feeding

Autumn feeding is where good intentions do damage.

Nitrogen pushes soft new top growth. Soft new top growth going into cold weather is the most vulnerable tissue the plant can have, and it also comes at the expense of the root and crown reserves the lawn should be building instead. You can end up with a lawn that looks greener in October and comes through winter in worse shape.

The general principle is to stop pushing growth well before dormancy and let the plant do what it is trying to do.

## Start staying ahead of leaves

This becomes the main autumn job, and it is worth starting before it becomes a thick layer.

Grass still needs light and air at the crowns. A mat of wet leaves sitting over the lawn blocks both and holds damp against the plant right as it is slowing down and least able to shrug it off.

A thin scatter can be mulched in with the mower and does the soil some good. A heavy layer is better cleared. The trick is not letting it get to the heavy stage in the first place — a quick pass now and then beats one enormous clear-up in November.

## Traffic matters more now

As growth slows, so does recovery. The lawn's ability to fill in a worn path goes away with the growing season, which means damage done in late autumn tends to stay visible until spring.

If there is a route across the yard that gets walked daily, or equipment that lives on the grass, autumn is the time to think about it. Anything you let wear now, you are looking at for months.

## What good autumn work buys you

None of this produces a visible result this season, which is why it gets skipped. The payoff shows up in spring: a lawn that went into dormancy with full reserves, good root depth, crowns that were not smothered and no worn paths cut into it greens up earlier, more evenly, and with fewer thin spots to fill.

Autumn is quiet work. It is also most of what decides how the lawn looks in April.
